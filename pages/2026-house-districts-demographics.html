Who Lives in Each House District

The United States House of Representatives has 435 voting districts, each holding roughly the same number of people — about 760,000 after the 2020 Census — and each electing one member for a two-year term. This page shows who lives in each district, from the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey (ACS) 2025 1-year estimates, and how each district voted in the House elections of 3 November 2026.

The map below is a tile cartogram: every district is one equal square, laid out to keep neighbouring districts neighbours as far as the grid allows. Pick a measure from the menu and the squares change colour along a green-to-orange scale — pale green at the low end, burnt orange at the high end. The chart underneath plots the same measure against each district’s Democratic vote share in 2026. Hover or tap a square or a dot to see the district’s numbers and its 2026 result.

Demographics as of the ACS 2025 1-year estimates (Census Bureau, published 29 October 2026), for the 119th Congress districts in use from 2023 to 2033. Election results as of 25 November 2026 — 432 of 435 races called; two Louisiana seats go to runoffs on 12 December and MN-01 awaits a hand recount. A “call” is a news organisation’s projection that a candidate has won, made before the count is finished, not an official result.

Sources: ACS 2025 1-year tables from the Census Bureau’s bulk summary files (Census Reporter had not yet ingested the 2025 release); 2026 House results from the 2026 midterm results page; 2024 House winners by district from Wikipedia’s 2024 House elections page (for the seats that changed party). Methods note

Where the numbers come from

  • Demographics: American Community Survey 2025 1-year estimates, Census Bureau, from the bulk summary files. The ACS surveys a sample of households every year; the 1-year estimates are the freshest, but for a single congressional district they carry margins of error of a few percent on most shares (larger on small groups). The page shows each estimate’s margin of error on hover. Shares are computed from the published table cells; percentage margins of error use the Census Bureau’s proportion formula, with a root-sum-of-squares fallback in the few cases where that formula’s radicand went negative. In the 2025 tables, B15003’s lines were renumbered (bachelor’s degree or higher is now lines 020–023); the 2025 release also discontinued household record swapping for 1-year products under the new disclosure-avoidance policy, so no noise-based protection was applied to these estimates and there is no comparability break with 2024 at district level.
  • Boundaries: 119th Congress (in use 2023–2033), from the 2020 Census cartographic boundary files. District populations are the 2020 Census CD119 counts.
  • Election results: 2026 House elections, as recorded on the 2026 midterm results page on 25 November 2026. The Democratic vote share is the winner’s share where a Democrat won, otherwise the runner-up’s share. Called districts only: 432 of 435, minus FL-10, which was called with no published vote totals, leaving 431 dots. The two Louisiana runoffs and MN-01 are not plotted.
  • Seats that changed party compare the 2026 winner with the 2024 winner by district, taken from Wikipedia’s 2024 House elections page (per-state results tables). 51 seats changed hands: 48 from Republican to Democratic, 3 from Democratic to Republican (ME-02, TN-09, TX-32). The 2024 totals reconcile exactly (R 220 / D 215), and the flips reconcile with the 2026 called tally (215 − 1 uncalled D seat − 3 + 48 = 259).
  • Colour: the tile map uses the site’s green-to-orange sequential ramp (pale green to burnt orange), in eight quantile bins — each bin holds roughly an eighth of the 435 districts. Party colours (blue Democrat, red Republican) are used only for election outcomes, never for demographics. District types in View 03 use a separate categorical palette of distinct hues, not the green-to-orange ramp, because types have no order.
  • District types (View 03): live clustering with e-means (Meta FAIR, MIT licence), ported from the e-means playground. e-means is a mini-batch form of k-means: it assigns districts to the nearest type centre in small batches and moves each centre by an exponential moving average, with the batch size and step size set by the scaling laws in the e-means paper (arXiv 2611.09501). The grouping runs in your browser, on z-scores — each measure rescaled to the 435-district mean and standard deviation — so dollars and percentage points count equally, with a fixed seed, so the same settings always give the same types. The four race and Hispanic-origin shares add to roughly 100%, so they move together; the grouping keeps all four, and the profiles should be read with that overlap in mind. District types are descriptive: two districts in one type are alike in demographics, which does not mean they vote alike. Vote columns in the profiles use called districts only.
